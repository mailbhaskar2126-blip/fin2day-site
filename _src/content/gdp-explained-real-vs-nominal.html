On 31 August, the government said India's economy grew 7.8% in April to June 2026. The next number, for July to September, is due on 30 November. It will be quoted everywhere as "GDP growth". Here is what that number actually counts, and how to read it.

What GDP is

Gross domestic product is the value of all final goods and services produced inside India in a period, usually a quarter or a year. "Final" matters: the steel in a car is counted once, inside the price of the car, not twice. "Inside India" matters too: a Korean carmaker's factory in Chennai counts; an Indian company's plant in Ohio does not.

India's statistics office, MoSPI, measures it two ways. One adds up what everyone spends. The other adds up what each sector produces, called gross value added or GVA. Both should arrive at roughly the same answer.

Real vs nominal: the most important gap

Nominal GDP is measured in today's rupees. If prices rise, nominal GDP rises even if the country makes nothing extra. Real GDP strips out price changes so you see the change in actual output. The headline 7.8% is real growth.

Illustration

An economy makes 100 shirts at ₹100 each: nominal GDP of ₹10,000. Next year it makes 105 shirts and the price rises to ₹105. Nominal GDP is now ₹11,025, up 10.25%. But output only rose 5%. That 5% is real growth; the rest is inflation.

India's GDP growth, April–June 2026. The deflator is the price change that separates the two. Source: MoSPI via Policy Edge and The Core.

The bridge between the two is the GDP deflator, a price index for everything the economy produces. In April to June it rose just 2.33%, even though wholesale prices averaged about 9.3% and consumer prices about 3.9% over those months, according to The Core. Some economists argue a low deflator can make real growth look stronger than it feels. Nominal growth also matters on its own: tax collections, company sales and the fiscal deficit ratio all move with nominal GDP.

Who is spending: the demand side

The spending method uses a famous formula: GDP = consumption + investment + government spending + exports − imports.

  • Private consumptionWhat households spend on food, rent, phones and travel. It was 55.6% of nominal GDP in April to June, the biggest piece by far.
  • Investment (GFCF)Gross fixed capital formation: factories, roads, machinery and homes. Its share rose to 34.3% of GDP from 31.4% a year earlier.
  • Government consumptionSalaries and day-to-day spending by government. Its building of roads counts under investment.
  • Net exportsExports add to GDP; imports subtract, because that spending went to output made abroad.
Real growth of GDP's spending components, April–June 2026 vs a year earlier. Source: MoSPI via Policy Edge and Business Today.

One quirk: real imports fell 1.1%, which mechanically lifts GDP. In rupee terms imports jumped 30.9%, mostly because oil and other import prices surged. Real export growth of 12.0% and investment growth of 11.9% did most of the lifting this quarter.

Who is producing: the supply side

GVA tracks output sector by sector. Add taxes on products and subtract subsidies, and you get GDP. In April to June, real GVA grew 8.2%.

Real GVA growth by sector, April–June 2026 vs a year earlier. Source: MoSPI via Policy Edge and Business Today.

Services led, with finance, real estate and IT up 12.1%. Farming grew a slower 3.6%. This split matters for jobs: agriculture still employs a large share of Indians, so strong headline growth can coexist with weak rural incomes.

How to read the 30 November release

Since February 2026, India's GDP series uses 2022-23 as its base year, replacing 2011-12. The base year sets the prices used for real GDP and the weights of each sector, so old and new numbers are not directly comparable. Past quarters are also revised; FY26 growth was revised to 7.8% from 7.7% in the August release.

Real GDP growth, recent quarters. The July–September bar is SBI Research's estimate (11 Nov 2026), not official data. Source: MoSPI via Business Today; SBI Research via Devdiscourse.

SBI Research expects July to September growth of about 7.8%, with GVA at 8% to 8.2% and nominal GDP at 11% to 11.5%. When the official figure lands, check four things:

  • Real and nominalIs the gap (the deflator) unusually small or large compared with inflation?
  • GDP vs GVAA big gap usually means tax or subsidy swings, not real activity.
  • ConsumptionHouseholds are more than half the economy. Investment can spike; consumption shows staying power.
  • RevisionsThe previous quarter's number often changes. Compare against the revised figure, not the old headline.

GDP is the broadest scorecard for the economy, and it feeds into decisions like the RBI's repo rate. But it measures output, not well-being or how evenly income is shared. Read it as one important number, not the whole story. Educational content only.
