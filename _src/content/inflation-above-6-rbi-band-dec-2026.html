On 12 November, the statistics ministry said retail inflation rose to 6.09% in October, from 5.54% in September. It was the ninth straight monthly rise, and it took inflation above 6%, the top of the range the RBI is legally asked to keep it in. SBI Research says it is the first time that has happened in the new CPI series.

Does that mean the RBI has missed its target? Not yet. Here's how the rules work, and why the next RBI meeting, ending on 4 December, matters so much.

The target: 4%, with room on either side

Since 2016, the RBI has worked under "flexible inflation targeting". Under Section 45-ZA of the RBI Act, the government sets a target every five years. In March 2026 it kept the target at 4%, with a tolerance band of 2 percentage points either way, for April 2026 to March 2031.

The band exists because inflation is noisy. A bad monsoon or an oil shock can push prices up for a few months without anything being wrong with monetary policy. So a single month above 6% is not a breach of the law. What counts is the quarterly average, and for how long.

Where inflation has been, quarter by quarter

Inflation averaged just over 3% in January–March and has climbed every quarter since. In its 7 October policy, the RBI projected the October–December quarter at 6.0%, right on the edge, before easing to 5.7% and 5.6% in the next two quarters. Those projections assumed crude at $95 a barrel.

When would the RBI officially "fail"?

The framework defines failure as average inflation above 6% (or below 2%) for three consecutive quarters. Only then must the RBI send the government a report explaining why, what it will do about it, and how long it expects inflation to take to return to target.

The three-quarter failure test, applied to the coming quarters. Source: RBI Act framework via Drishti IAS; RBI projections via Groundamentals.

So the earliest a formal failure could be declared is after June 2027, and only if inflation stays above 6% for all of October 2026 to June 2027. The RBI's own forecasts say it won't. But October has already started the quarter above the line.

The arithmetic for this quarter

SBI Research and Union Bank of India both estimate November inflation at about 6.3%. These are tracking estimates, not official data; the November figure is due on 14 December. If they are right, December would need to come in at roughly 5.6% or lower for the quarter to average 6% or less.

What's driving it

  • FoodFood inflation rose to 8.94% from 7.74%. Onion prices were up 116.22% from a year ago, garlic 60.74% and ginger 48.32%. Potatoes were 14.24% cheaper.
  • VillagesRural inflation was 6.63%, urban 5.41%. Rural food inflation was 9.09%. More in our September inflation explainer.
  • Wholesale costsWholesale inflation was 12.29% in October, with fuel and power up 32.84%. Higher factory costs can reach shop prices with a lag.
  • CoreInflation excluding food and fuel was 4.75%, up from 4.60%, according to SBI Research. That is above the 4% target, but well below the headline.

Why a December hike looks likely

The repo rate is 5.50% after the RBI's 25 basis point hike on 7 October, its first increase in about three years (see why the RBI raised rates). With inflation at 6.09%, the repo rate is now below inflation, a "real" rate of about −0.6% (FIN2DAY calculation). Central banks usually want real rates above zero when they are trying to cool prices.

Analyst forecasts for the repo rate, November 2026. Source: Union Bank of India via MENAFN; Choice Institutional Equities via IANS, cited by Trading and Investment News.

Union Bank of India expects a 25 basis point hike to 5.75% on 4 December. Choice Institutional Equities expects hikes in both December and February, taking the rate to about 6.25%. ICRA's Aditi Nayar has said another 25 basis point hike "appears almost certain". SBI Research now sees a 25 basis point move as more likely than the 50 it had considered earlier.

There are softer signals too. SBI Research notes that Brent crude has fallen about 11%, the rupee has gained 0.7% against the dollar, and foreign investors have put roughly $7.68 billion into India since 4 November. Those could ease inflation in early 2027 and give the RBI reason to move slowly.

What it means for you

A December hike would most quickly reach floating-rate loans linked to the repo rate; see how the October hike reached EMIs and FDs. Fixed deposit rates usually follow more slowly. Meanwhile, at 6% inflation, a savings account or FD paying less than that is losing value in real terms.

4 December: RBI policy decision. 14 December: November CPI. Together they will show whether October was a peak or the start of a longer stay above 6%. Educational content only, not investment advice.
