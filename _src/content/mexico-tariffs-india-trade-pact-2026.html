When people in India talk about tariffs, they usually mean the United States. But since 1 January 2026, another big market has been charging Indian goods a lot more: Mexico.

On 28 October 2026, India's Commerce Secretary Rajesh Agrawal and Mexico's Undersecretary of Foreign Trade, Luis Rosendo Gutiérrez Romano, signed the terms of reference for a preferential trade agreement (PTA) in Mexico City. That's the formal start of negotiations. Here's what led to it and what's at stake.

What Mexico did

In December 2025, Mexico's Senate approved a law raising import duties on about 1,400 product lines, by 76 votes to 5, with 35 abstentions. The new rates range from 5% to 50%, with most products facing up to 35%. They apply only to countries that have no trade agreement with Mexico. That list includes China, India, South Korea, Thailand and Indonesia.

Mexico gave two reasons: protecting local industry and jobs, and raising money. It expected about $3.76 billion in extra revenue in 2026. The duties also fit the "Mexico Plan" launched in January 2025, which aims to raise local content in Mexican supply chains by 15% by 2030. Analysts and business groups quoted by The Tribune also saw a political motive: keeping the US happy ahead of the review of the US–Mexico–Canada trade pact, at a time when Washington wants Latin America to buy less from China.

So India wasn't the main target. But it was caught in the net.

What India sells to Mexico

India exported goods worth $5.73 billion to Mexico in 2025-26. That's a small slice of India's total exports, about 1.3% in 2024-25, according to Business Standard. But it's concentrated in a few industries. Engineering goods made up 61% of the total, and automobiles alone were worth $1.92 billion in 2025-26, the largest single category.

India's biggest export categories to Mexico. Vehicles and parts dominate. Source: GTRI, via The Tribune.

The think tank GTRI estimated that nearly 75% of India's exports to Mexico would face higher duties.

Cars took the biggest hit

Mexico is India's third-largest market for car exports, after South Africa and Saudi Arabia. Indian-made cars accounted for 6.7% of Mexico's passenger vehicle sales, according to Business Standard, mostly small cars. Skoda/Volkswagen made nearly half of India's car shipments to Mexico, followed by Hyundai (about $200 million), Nissan (about $140 million) and Suzuki (about $120 million), according to Reuters data cited by Business Standard.

The duty on passenger vehicles rose from 20% to 50%. Many other Indian products jumped to 35%.

Mexico's new duties on goods from non-agreement countries, with the old rate in brackets. Cars per Business Standard; other rates per GTRI estimates.

What does a jump from 20% to 50% mean in practice? Take a car that lands at the Mexican border with a value of $10,000.

Illustration: the same car costs 25% more at the border after the duty hike, before Mexico's other taxes and dealer margins.

That gap is hard to absorb through discounts alone. One carmaker told Business Standard it could serve Mexico from factories in other countries instead. At the time, FIEO's Director-General Ajay Sahai said the hike had "come at the wrong time", since Indian exporters were then also facing high US tariffs. (Those US duties have since come down; see our explainer on US tariffs on India.)

Why India negotiated instead of hitting back

In the classic trade war, a country answers a tariff with a tariff, as China did in the US–China fight of 2018–19. India has chosen not to, and the numbers explain why.

India sells Mexico far more than it buys. FY2025-26 figures, Ministry of Commerce data via Fibre2Fashion. Surplus: FIN2DAY calculation.

India bought only about $2.1 billion of goods from Mexico in 2025-26. Raising duties on that would give India little leverage and could hurt its own importers. GTRI made this point when the tariffs were announced, saying retaliation was unlikely. A deal, on the other hand, is the only route that cuts the duty: Mexico's higher rates apply to countries without a trade agreement.

What the talks cover

  • ScopeA PTA is narrower than a full free trade agreement. It cuts duties on a selected list of goods rather than almost everything.
  • Early harvestCommerce Minister Piyush Goyal has said Mexico wants an "early harvest": duty cuts on some products before the full deal is done.
  • India's prioritiesAutomobiles, textiles, footwear, plastics, steel, chemicals and toys are among the sectors exposed to the higher duties.
  • No cuts yetSigning the terms of reference sets out what will be negotiated. It doesn't reduce any duty by itself, and no deadline has been announced.

Agrawal said the agreement is a chance to "build bridges across the Pacific towards shared prosperity". India is negotiating on several fronts at once: talks with Chile are close to a conclusion, the sixth round with Canada is expected in early November, and talks with the Southern African Customs Union have started.

The bigger picture

Mexico's tariffs show how trade fights now spread. Washington pressured its neighbour over Chinese goods, Mexico raised walls against everyone without a deal, and India got hit along the way.
