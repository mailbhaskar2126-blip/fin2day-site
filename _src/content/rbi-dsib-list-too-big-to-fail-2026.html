On Monday, 23 November, the Reserve Bank of India published its 2026 list of Domestic Systemically Important Banks, or D-SIBs. The names were the same as last year: State Bank of India, HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank. None of them moved bucket, the RBI said, and the assessment used data the banks reported as of 31 March 2026.

It is a short press release that rarely makes headlines. But it decides how much extra shareholder money India's three biggest lenders must keep as a cushion, and it is the RBI's official answer to an awkward question: which banks are too big to be allowed to fail?

What "systemically important" means

A bank is systemically important when its failure would hurt far more than its own customers. Millions of salaries, loans, payments and other banks' deposits run through it. If such a bank got into trouble, the government would come under huge pressure to rescue it.

That expectation is itself a problem. If lenders and depositors believe a bank will always be bailed out, it can borrow more cheaply and may be tempted to take bigger risks. Regulators call this moral hazard. After the 2008 global financial crisis, the Basel Committee asked countries to identify their largest banks and make them hold more capital. The RBI issued India's framework on 22 July 2014 and updated it on 28 December 2023.

How India's D-SIB list has evolved. Source: RBI, via Upstox and Business Standard.

How the RBI decides

The RBI gives each large bank a Systemic Importance Score. According to the coverage of the framework, the score looks at:

  • SizeHow big the bank's balance sheet is. IASPoint reports that banks with assets above 2% of India's GDP are included in the assessment.
  • ConnectionsHow interconnected it is: how much it borrows from, and lends to, other banks and financial firms.
  • SubstitutabilityHow hard it would be for others to take over its role in payments, lending and other services.
  • ComplexityHow complicated its business is, including activities abroad.

Banks above the cut-off are placed in one of five buckets. The higher the bucket, the more extra capital the bank must hold.

Extra Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) capital required in each D-SIB bucket, and the bank placed there in 2026. Buckets 3 and 5 are empty. Source: RBI 2026 D-SIB list, via HelloBanker and IASPoint.

SBI sits in bucket 4 and must hold an extra 0.80% of its risk-weighted assets as CET1 capital. HDFC Bank is in bucket 2 (0.40%) and ICICI Bank in bucket 1 (0.20%). SBI was first named in 2015, ICICI Bank in 2016 and HDFC Bank in 2017.

Where the surcharge fits

CET1 is the highest-quality capital a bank has: mostly shareholders' equity and retained profits, money that can absorb losses without the bank defaulting. Under the RBI's capital rules, every commercial bank must hold CET1 of at least 5.5% of risk-weighted assets, plus a 2.5% capital conservation buffer, also in CET1. That makes 8%. The RBI says the D-SIB surcharge comes on top of the conservation buffer.

Minimum CET1 capital including the conservation buffer and the D-SIB surcharge, % of risk-weighted assets. FIN2DAY calculation from RBI rules.

"Risk-weighted" matters. Cash carries a zero weight, while ordinary loans are typically counted at full value. So a bank that lends more, or lends to riskier borrowers, needs more capital in rupee terms.

Capital held as a cushion cannot be lent out. Shareholders' money is also more expensive than deposits, so the surcharge slightly raises a big bank's cost of doing business. That is the point: the extra cost offsets the funding advantage that comes from being seen as too big to fail.

Foreign banks in India that are on the global list of systemically important banks (G-SIBs) face a similar rule. They must hold the extra CET1 required of them as a G-SIB, in proportion to their risk-weighted assets in India.

Does this mean my deposits are guaranteed?

No. The label is a regulatory classification that brings stricter capital rules and closer supervision. It is not a promise of a bailout, and it says nothing about whether a bank's shares are a good investment.

Why it matters now

The list lands during a demanding stretch for banks. The RBI raised the repo rate in October, as covered in our explainer on the hike, and bank cash has been tight, as we explained in India's bank cash squeeze. Higher rates are now reaching loans and FDs. The D-SIB surcharge is one of the ways the RBI makes sure the three banks at the centre of that system have extra room to absorb losses if conditions get worse.
