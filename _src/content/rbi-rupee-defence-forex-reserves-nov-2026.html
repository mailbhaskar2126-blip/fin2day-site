In the first week of September 2026, India's foreign exchange reserves hit a record $785.71 billion. Eight weeks later, on 30 October, they stood at $717.06 billion, down about $68.65 billion. Much of that money went into one job: stopping the rupee from falling through 97 to the dollar.

For now, it seems to be working. The rupee closed at ₹93.905 on 6 November, its strongest level since 22 April. Here's how the RBI did it, and what it cost.

Eight weeks of falling reserves

India's foreign exchange reserves, US$ billion. September weeks between 4 Sep and 2 Oct are not shown. The 16 Oct figure is derived from the 23 Oct level and its reported weekly change. Source: RBI data via PTI, ANI, Business Today.

Reserves have fallen for eight weeks in a row. The biggest falls came in September and early October, including $12.95 billion in the week to 2 October and $8.58 billion in the week to 9 October. The pace has since slowed: $1.27 billion and $2.69 billion in the two latest reported weeks.

Reserves can also fall when gold prices drop or when the euro and yen weaken against the dollar, because those holdings are valued in dollars. But PTI reported that the latest declines were driven partly by the RBI's dollar sales to support the rupee, and partly by banks using dollars raised under the RBI's special FCNR(B) deposit swap scheme earlier this year.

What the reserves are made of

Components of India's foreign exchange reserves, week ended 30 October 2026. Source: RBI data via National Herald (PTI).

Only the first bar is real ammunition. Foreign currency assets, mostly US Treasury bills and deposits with other central banks, are what the RBI can sell when it wants to support the rupee. Gold, Special Drawing Rights (SDRs) from the IMF and India's IMF reserve position are much harder to use quickly. Foreign currency assets fell to $591.12 billion on 30 October.

The 10 October toolkit

On 9 October the rupee closed at ₹96.73, close to its record low of ₹96.96 set in May. Days after raising the repo rate to 5.50%, the RBI announced a package aimed directly at dollar demand.

  • Oil dollar windowFrom 12 October, the RBI sells dollars to Indian Oil, HPCL and BPCL through designated banks, to meet their entire daily dollar needs. Their crude purchases no longer hit the open market.
  • Proof of exposureCompanies could earlier hedge up to $100 million without proving an underlying trade. That limit was cut to $5 million, and hedgers must declare they haven't hedged the same exposure with another bank.
  • A 20% cash depositFor contracts above $2 million where the user buys dollars against rupees, banks must keep 20% of the rupee value as cash with the RBI until the contract ends. This is the foreign exchange risk reserve, or FERR.
  • No rebookingA cancelled rupee derivative contract can no longer be rebooked, which stops traders from repeatedly cancelling and re-entering bets.

The common thread: make it harder and costlier to buy dollars for speculation, while leaving genuine trade alone.

Illustration: how the 20% FERR works on a $10 million dollar-buying contract at ₹94 to the dollar. FIN2DAY calculation.

That ₹18.8 crore earns nothing while it sits with the RBI, according to DMI Finance's reading of the rules, which have not said the balances are paid interest. DMI estimates the carrying cost at about 1.10 to 1.15 percentage points a year. For a speculator, that eats into any profit. For a genuine importer, it's an extra cost of hedging.

Did it work?

How many rupees per dollar stronger than its May record low of ₹96.96 the rupee closed on each date. FIN2DAY calculation. Sources: Business Today, Free Press Journal, National Herald (PTI).

The rupee gained 1.9% in the week after the measures, closing at ₹94.96 on 16 October. By 6 November it was at ₹93.905. It is still about 4.5% weaker against the dollar than at the start of 2026, according to PTI.

At the 7 October policy, the Governor said reserves "continue to remain adequate in terms of standard metrics of reserve adequacy" and that the RBI was committed to "orderly adjustments" in the exchange rate. Not everyone is convinced the calm will last. In an opinion piece for The Quint, Subhash Chandra Garg argued that the rupee crossing ₹100 to the dollar in 2026-27 "remains the most likely case", citing continuing outflows and the RBI's forward commitments.

The side effect: less cash in banks

When the RBI sells dollars, it takes rupees back in exchange. That drains cash from the banking system. The same 10 October package also included a ₹25,000 crore sale of government bonds and a rule that banks keep 99% of their required cash reserve every day, up from 90%. Together, these explain much of the squeeze in bank liquidity.

What to watch

The RBI publishes reserves every Friday. A return to weekly gains would suggest the pressure has eased. Watch oil too: a cheaper barrel means fewer dollars for the oil companies' window. See how the Fed and foreign selling pushed the rupee down. Educational content only.
