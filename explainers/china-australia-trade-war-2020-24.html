China vs Australia, 2020–24: an 80.5% barley duty, 218% on wine, and a coal windfall for India

After Australia called for a COVID-19 inquiry, China hit its wine, barley, coal and beef. Most of the trade found new buyers within months, and Indian coal users were among the winners.

In 2020, Australia asked for an international inquiry into the origins of COVID-19. Within months, China, its biggest customer, hit Australian barley with an 80.5% duty, wine with duties of up to 218%, and quietly told buyers to stop taking Australian coal, cotton and lobster. It became one of the clearest modern cases of a country using trade as punishment. It also had an unexpected winner: Indian coal buyers.

What China targeted

Relations had been sliding since 2018, when Canberra kept Huawei out of its 5G network. The inquiry call in 2020 tipped them over. Between May and November 2020, Beijing rolled out a mix of formal and informal measures:

  • BarleyAnti-dumping and anti-subsidy duties totalling 80.5%, set for five years. The trade had been worth A$1.5 billion to A$2 billion a year.
  • WineDuties of up to 218% for some brands. China was Australia's top wine market, worth A$1.1 billion in 2019.
  • CoalAn unofficial order in October 2020 to avoid Australian coal. Dozens of ships were left stranded outside Chinese ports.
  • Beef, cotton, lobster, timberSuspended meat plants, a 40% tariff threat to cotton mills, extra inspections and "biosecurity" bans.
Yearly value of selected Australian exports to China before the curbs, A$ billion. Years differ by product; barley is the low end of the A$1.5–2 billion a year Reuters cites. Source: Reuters (via Devdiscourse), Australian government (via Al Jazeera).

The pressure was real because Australia leaned heavily on China. In 2020, China bought 33% of everything Australia exported, according to Australian government data cited by the United States Studies Centre at the University of Sydney.

Where the goods went instead

The surprise of this trade war is how much of the blocked trade simply moved. Most of these goods are commodities, and commodities can find another buyer if the price is right.

Coal is the best example, and it runs straight through India. Turned away from China, Australian coal was offered at steep discounts. Indian cement makers and power users bought it. In February 2021, India became the second-biggest buyer of Australian thermal coal. Australia's share of India's thermal coal imports jumped from a little over 4% on average in 2020 to around 20% in the first two months of 2021, Reuters reported, citing trade data.

Australia's share of India's thermal coal imports, %. Source: Reuters (via The Business Standard), citing Lavi Coal Info, April 2021.

Argus, a commodity price agency, reported a record 2.46 million tonnes of Australian thermal coal shipped to India in April 2021 alone, the fourth straight month with no shipments to China. Meanwhile, Chinese importers paid more for lower-quality coal from Indonesia, Russia and Mongolia.

Other goods followed the same path. Barley went to the Middle East, though at lower prices. Cotton went to Vietnam, Indonesia and Thailand. Copper went to Japan and South Korea.

The redirection was not free. Australia's total coal exports fell by more than 7%, from 213 million tonnes in 2019-20 to 198 million tonnes in 2020-21, Reuters reported. China's share of Australia's coal exports fell from 20% in 2019 to 9% in 2023.

Wine: the product that could not escape

Wine was different. A branded bottle of Australian red is not a commodity, and Chinese drinkers had no easy substitute in other markets. Sales to Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand rose, but not enough. Australia's total wine exports fell by 30% between 2020 and 2022.

Australian wine as a share of China's wine imports, %. Source: Al Jazeera, 28 March 2024.

Australian wine went from 27.5% of China's wine imports in 2020 to just 0.14% in the first half of 2023. That is close to a total shutdown of what had been a billion-dollar market.

How it ended

A new Australian government took office in 2022 and took a calmer line with Beijing. From early 2023, China began lifting the curbs: coal, cotton, timber and copper resumed. The barley duties ended on 5 August 2023, and Australia dropped its WTO case over them. The wine duties went on 29 March 2024, and Australia ended that WTO case too.

Illustration: key steps in the China–Australia trade dispute. Source: United States Studies Centre, Reuters, Al Jazeera.

Wine sales bounced back quickly. Between April and December 2024, Australia shipped 83 million litres of wine worth A$902 million to mainland China, according to Wine Australia data reported by The Drinks Business. Chinese wine drinking, however, is still below where it was before the tariffs.

What it means for India

Three lessons stand out.

  • Concentration is riskA country that sells a third of its exports to one buyer gives that buyer leverage. India's own trade with China is heavily one-sided the other way, as a big importer, which brings a different kind of dependence.
  • Commodities reroute, brands sufferCoal and barley found new buyers within months. Wine, which depends on taste and brand, did not.
  • Third countries can gainWhen two trading partners fight, a bystander can buy at a discount. In 2021 that bystander was India, buying cheaper Australian coal.

The same pattern shows up in the US–China trade war of 2018–19, where other Asian exporters picked up business. For India's current trade fights, see the swings in US tariffs on India and the bulk Scotch dispute with the UK.
