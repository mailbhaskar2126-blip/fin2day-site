Fiscal deficit, explained: why the government borrows over ₹4,600 crore a day

The Centre has used 42% of this year's deficit target in six months. What the fiscal deficit measures, why most new borrowing pays old interest, and why it matters for loan rates.

On 30 October, the government's accountants released a number that rarely makes the front page: the Centre's fiscal deficit for April to September 2026 was ₹7.14 lakh crore. That is 42% of the target for the whole year, against about 37% at the same point last year.

The fiscal deficit is one of the most quoted numbers in Indian economics, and one of the least explained. Here is what it is, how it is measured, and why it matters for your loan rates.

What the fiscal deficit is

The fiscal deficit is the gap between what the central government spends in a year and what it earns without borrowing. Earnings here mean taxes, dividends and fees, plus small capital receipts like loan recoveries and stake sales. Whatever is left uncovered has to be borrowed. So the fiscal deficit is, in effect, the government's new borrowing for the year.

It is usually shown as a percentage of GDP, the size of the whole economy. A ₹17 lakh crore gap means little on its own; as a share of what the country produces, it can be compared across years and countries.

The 2026-27 Budget in one picture

In February, the Budget planned total spending of ₹53.47 lakh crore against non-debt receipts of about ₹36.5 lakh crore. The gap, the fiscal deficit, was set at ₹16.95 lakh crore, or 4.3% of GDP.

FIN2DAY calculation from Budget 2026-27 figures. Rounded to the nearest rupee.

Put simply, nearly a third of every rupee the Centre spends this year is borrowed. That works out to roughly ₹4,646 crore of new borrowing every day (FIN2DAY calculation). Most of it is raised by selling government bonds: gross market borrowing is budgeted at ₹17.2 lakh crore, including money to repay old bonds as they mature.

Illustration: a household version

Imagine a family that earns ₹68,000 a month but spends ₹1,00,000. It borrows ₹32,000 to fill the gap. Now imagine ₹26,000 of that spending is interest on earlier loans. That is roughly the shape of the Centre's 2026-27 Budget.

Three deficits, three questions

  • Fiscal deficitTotal spending minus non-debt receipts. How much is the government borrowing this year?
  • Revenue deficitDay-to-day spending (salaries, subsidies, interest) minus revenue receipts. Is it borrowing just to pay running costs?
  • Primary deficitFiscal deficit minus interest payments. Leaving out the cost of past borrowing, is it still spending more than it earns?

Borrowing to build roads and railways, which can lift future growth, is viewed more kindly than borrowing to pay salaries. That is why economists watch the revenue deficit and the mix of spending, not just the headline.

The interest trap

The biggest single item in the Budget is interest on past borrowing: ₹14.04 lakh crore in 2026-27, about 39.7% of the Centre's revenue receipts. It is larger than the whole capital spending budget of ₹12.22 lakh crore.

Budget 2026-27 estimates. Primary deficit = fiscal deficit minus interest (FIN2DAY calculation).

Interest alone is about 83% of this year's fiscal deficit (FIN2DAY calculation). The primary deficit is only around ₹2.91 lakh crore. In other words, most new borrowing is needed to service old borrowing. This is why governments care about cutting the deficit: every year of high borrowing raises the interest bill for years afterwards.

How India got here

The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act of 2003 set targets for the deficit and debt. A 2018 amendment made debt-to-GDP the main anchor. Then Covid hit: in 2020-21 the deficit jumped to 9.3% of GDP as tax revenue fell and relief spending rose.

Centre's fiscal deficit, % of GDP. CGA provisional figures for each year; 2026-27 is the Budget target.

Since then the government has trimmed the deficit every year. It met its 4.4% target for 2025-26, with a provisional deficit of ₹15.19 lakh crore. The new goal is to cut central government debt from 56.1% of GDP (2025-26 revised estimate) to 55.6% this year, and to 50%, give or take one point, by 2030-31.

Why the first half of 2026-27 is a worry

Hitting 4.3% this year looks harder. By end-September, the government had used 42% of its full-year deficit allowance, compared with about 37% a year earlier.

April–September 2026, % of 2026-27 Budget estimate used. Source: CGA via Business Standard.

Spending is running ahead: subsidies have used 54% of their budget, led by fertilisers, as costs rose after the West Asia war. Receipts are lagging. Net tax revenue grew only about 4%. Excise duty fell 22%, partly because the government cut the special additional excise duty on petrol and diesel by ₹10 a litre in late March. ICRA's chief economist Aditi Nayar said meeting the target "may prove challenging" and that receipts would need to grow 16.2% in the second half.

Capital spending, by contrast, is at 54% of its target, though it fell about 30% in August and 3% in September. If revenue keeps falling short, capex is often the easiest thing for a government to cut.

Why it matters to you

When the government borrows more, it competes with companies and households for the same pool of savings. More bond supply can push up government bond yields, and higher yields tend to feed into loan and deposit rates. A widening deficit can also make the RBI's job harder at a time when it has already raised rates. For how those rates reach your EMI, see what the repo rate is.

The full-year verdict will come in May 2027, when the Controller General of Accounts publishes provisional figures for 2026-27.
