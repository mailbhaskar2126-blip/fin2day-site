Mutual fund expense ratios, explained: how a 1% fee can cost you ₹14 lakh

Every mutual fund quietly takes a cut of your money each year. What the expense ratio is, why direct plans cost less, and what SEBI's new 2026 caps changed.

When you invest in a mutual fund, you never get a bill. There is no monthly invoice and no line on your bank statement. Yet every fund charges you every single day. The charge is called the expense ratio, and over a long investment it can matter as much as which fund you pick.

What the expense ratio is

The expense ratio is the share of a fund's assets that is used to run it each year. If a fund has an expense ratio of 1%, roughly ₹1 of every ₹100 you have invested goes to costs over a year.

It is not deducted all at once. The fund takes a tiny slice every day, and the net asset value (NAV) you see is already after that slice. That is why most investors never notice it: the returns a fund reports are always net of its expenses.

The money pays for the fund manager and research team, the distributor's commission (in regular plans), the registrar that keeps investor records, the custodian that holds the securities, audit, and marketing.

What changed in April 2026

SEBI caps how much a fund can charge. Until March 2026, the cap was on the total expense ratio (TER), which also included taxes and levies. From 1 April 2026, under the new mutual fund regulations, the cap applies to a narrower base expense ratio (BER). GST, securities transaction tax (STT), commodity transaction tax, stamp duty and SEBI and exchange fees now sit outside the cap and are charged on top.

The new structure: TER = base expense ratio + brokerage and exchange costs + statutory levies. Source: Cafemutual, Kotak Mutual Fund.

The caps themselves came down, by up to 0.15 percentage points. Brokerage that funds can charge to the scheme was also cut, from 12 basis points to 5 for cash-market trades and from 2 to 1 for derivatives. (One basis point is 0.01%.)

Maximum expense ratio for open-ended equity funds, by fund size. Grey: old TER cap. Dark: new BER cap from 1 April 2026. Source: SEBI, via Cafemutual.

Bigger funds must charge less, because costs are spread over more money. An equity fund with up to ₹500 crore can charge up to 2.10%; above ₹50,000 crore, the cap on the extra assets is 0.95%. Debt and other non-equity funds have lower caps, from 1.85% down to 0.70%. Index funds and ETFs are capped at 0.90%.

The catch: because taxes and levies now come on top, the total you pay has not always fallen. Outlook Money tracked 43 flexi-cap schemes and found that every one of them reported a higher TER in May 2026 than in March 2026, with direct plans generally hit harder than regular plans.

Direct vs regular: same fund, different price

Almost every fund comes in two versions. A regular plan is bought through a distributor or adviser, who is paid a commission out of the expense ratio. A direct plan is bought straight from the fund house or an execution-only platform, with no commission. Both hold exactly the same shares and are run by the same manager.

The gap between the two is often between 0.5 and 1.5 percentage points a year. Across the 43 flexi-cap funds in Outlook Money's sample, regular plans cost on average 1.14 percentage points more than direct plans in March 2026.

Why a small number gets big

A one-point difference sounds trivial. But the fee is charged on your whole balance, every year, including on the gains from earlier years. It compounds against you, just as returns compound for you (see how compounding works in a SIP).

Illustration: ₹10 lakh invested once in the same fund, assuming a 12% a year return before fees, with a 0.5% vs 1.5% annual expense ratio. FIN2DAY calculation.

In this illustration, ₹10 lakh grows to ₹88.21 lakh after 20 years with a 0.5% fee, and to ₹73.66 lakh with a 1.5% fee. The one-point difference costs ₹14.55 lakh, more than the original investment.

Illustration: how much of a 20-year corpus is lost to fees, compared with a fund that charges nothing, assuming a 12% a year return before fees. FIN2DAY calculation.

At 2% a year, nearly a third of what you could have had goes to costs over 20 years. At 0.2%, typical of many low-cost index funds, it is about 3.5%.

How to read it

  • Where to find itFund houses publish the current TER of every plan on their websites, and AMFI collects them. It can change during the year, so check the latest figure, not an old factsheet.
  • Compare like with likeA liquid fund will always be cheaper than a small-cap fund. Compare a fund's cost with others in the same category, and direct with direct.
  • Cheaper is not automatically betterAn active fund that beats its benchmark after fees can be worth a higher cost. But fees are certain and outperformance is not. Index funds, which simply copy an index like the Nifty 50 (see how the Nifty is calculated), usually cost the least.
  • Advice has value tooA regular plan's commission pays for a distributor's service. If that help keeps you investing through a falling market, it may be worth paying for. The point is to know what you pay.
The bottom line

The expense ratio is the one part of a fund's return you know in advance. Over decades, a fraction of a percent a year adds up to lakhs of rupees. Educational content only, not investment advice.
