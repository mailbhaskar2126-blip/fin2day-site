Real returns, explained: why a 6.25% FD now earns you almost nothing

With inflation at 6.09%, the interest on your deposit barely keeps up with prices. What real return means, how to calculate it, and how tax makes it negative.

Your fixed deposit statement shows a number like 6.25%. That is the nominal return: how many more rupees you get back. But what you really care about is what those rupees can buy. That is the real return, and in India today it is much thinner than the headline rate.

The one-line idea

Real return is roughly the interest rate minus inflation. If your FD pays 6.25% and prices rise 6.09% in a year, you end the year with 6.25% more rupees, but each rupee buys about 6% less. Your gain in buying power is close to zero.

The exact formula

Real return = (1 + interest rate) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1. At 6.25% and 6.09%, that is 1.0625 ÷ 1.0609 − 1 = 0.15%. The quick "subtract" method gives 0.16%. At low rates the two barely differ.

Where savers stand right now

Retail inflation was 6.09% in October, according to the statistics ministry, up from 5.54% in September. Food prices rose 8.94%. Against that, State Bank of India pays 6.25% on a one-year FD and 2.50% on savings accounts. Senior citizens get 6.75% on the one-year FD. Bank of India's 500-day non-callable special deposit pays 7.00%, among the highest public sector bank offers listed in November.

Real return on common deposits, using October 2026 CPI inflation of 6.09%, before tax. FIN2DAY calculation. Rates: SBI and Bank of India via Upstox and BankBazaar; inflation: MoSPI.

Money sitting in a savings account is losing more than 3% of its buying power a year. A regular FD is roughly treading water.

One caveat: this is a snapshot. Inflation figures look backward over the past 12 months, while an FD rate applies to the year ahead. Your actual real return depends on what inflation does during your deposit, which nobody knows in advance.

Same FD, very different year

SBI's one-year rate has stayed at 6.25% all year. What changed is inflation. In January it was just 2.74%, so the same FD earned a real return of about 3.4%. By October that had shrunk to 0.15%.

SBI one-year FD rate vs CPI inflation, January to October 2026. The gap between the lines is roughly the real return. October inflation is provisional. Source: MoSPI; SBI rates via BankBazaar and Upstox.

That is why the RBI's rate decisions matter to savers. The RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% on 7 October, and banks have started lifting some deposit rates, mostly through special tenures. We tracked that in how the hike is reaching loans and FDs. Until deposit rates catch up with inflation, real returns stay thin.

Then comes tax

FD interest is added to your income under "Income from Other Sources" and taxed at your slab rate, in both the old and new regimes. Banks deduct 10% TDS once interest crosses a threshold, but that is only an advance; your final tax depends on your slab.

Tax is charged on the full nominal interest, including the part that only makes up for inflation. So after tax, real returns can turn negative even when the headline rate beats inflation.

Illustration (FIN2DAY calculation): real return on a 6.25% FD after tax at different slab rates, with inflation at 6.09%. Health and education cess, deductions and rebates are ignored. Tax treatment: Business Today.

Under the new regime, income up to ₹12 lakh carries no tax after the rebate, so many savers effectively pay nothing. For someone in the 30% slab, the same FD loses about 1.6% of buying power a year.

Why small gaps grow large

Inflation compounds, just like returns do. A gap of a couple of percentage points a year looks harmless, but over long periods it decides whether savings keep their value.

Illustration (FIN2DAY calculation): what ₹1 lakh would buy in today's money if prices rise 4% or 6% every year. 4% is the RBI's inflation target. Not a forecast.

At 6% inflation, prices roughly double in 12 years. Money that earns less than inflation for that long would buy only about half as much. That is the logic behind long-term investing in assets that can beat inflation, a theme we covered in how a SIP works, though those assets come with ups and downs of their own.

How to use the idea

  • Compare like with likeLook at any return next to inflation. "8% a year" means little without knowing that prices rose 6%.
  • Think after taxCompare what you keep after tax with inflation, not the headline rate.
  • Match the horizonOver a few months, safety and access often matter more than real return. Over decades, real return matters most.
  • Watch the RBIThe RBI aims for 4% inflation. Why it is above 6% now, and what happens next, is in our inflation explainer.
