The US–Japan chip war of the 1980s: 100% tariffs, a secret 20% target, and Korea's win

In 1987 Washington slapped 100% tariffs on Japanese electronics to enforce a chip deal. Japan did open its market, but the real winner of the fight was a country that wasn't in it.

On 17 April 1987, US President Ronald Reagan put 100% tariffs on about $300 million of Japanese electronics. The fight was over computer chips, and it holds lessons for every trade war since, including the one India is living through now.

How Japan took the lead

In the late 1970s, American firms led the world in semiconductors. Within a decade, Japanese companies had overtaken them, especially in DRAM, the memory chips inside every computer.

Share of the world market for mass-produced semiconductors, 1978 vs 1987. Source: Wikipedia, 1986 U.S.–Japan Semiconductor Agreement.

In DRAM alone, the US share fell from about 70% in 1978 to 20% in 1986, while Japan's rose to 75%. American chipmakers made two complaints. First, that Japanese firms were "dumping" chips abroad, selling them below cost to win market share. Second, that Japan's own market was closed: foreign chips held only about 8.5% of it.

In June 1985, the US Semiconductor Industry Association filed a petition under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, the law that lets the US president retaliate against unfair trade practices. By then America's overall trade deficit with Japan had passed $40 billion a year.

The deal, and its secret letter

On 2 September 1986, the two governments signed the Semiconductor Agreement. Japan promised two things:

  • No dumpingJapan's trade ministry, MITI, would monitor the prices Japanese firms charged for chips, in the US and in other countries.
  • Market accessJapan would help foreign chipmakers sell more in Japan. The public text named no number, but a confidential side letter mentioned a goal of "around 20%" within five years.

That secret 20% target was unusual. Most trade deals set rules; this one, in effect, set a result.

The tariffs

Within months, Washington said Japan wasn't keeping its word: chips were still being sold cheaply in third countries, and the foreign share of Japan's market hadn't moved. On 27 March 1987, Reagan announced tariffs on "$300 million in Japanese exports," citing "Japan's inability to enforce" the agreement. The duties took effect on 17 April.

The targets weren't chips themselves but finished goods that contained them, such as laptop computers, colour televisions and power tools. The idea was to hurt Japanese exporters roughly as much as they were thought to have gained by breaking the deal.

Japanese goods facing the 100% tariff, as parts were lifted in 1987. FIN2DAY calculation from figures in The Fab, chapter 16; American Presidency Project.

The sanctions were designed to be lifted as Japan complied. Once dumping eased, $51 million was lifted in June 1987 and another $84 million in November, leaving roughly half in place.

Did it work?

On its own terms, partly. The foreign share of Japan's chip market climbed slowly, and the 20% goal was finally reached at the end of 1992. The agreement was renewed in 1991 and replaced by a looser deal in 1996.

Foreign chipmakers' share of the Japanese semiconductor market. Sources: Christian Science Monitor (1987 figure), Wikipedia.

But the price controls had a side effect. With Japanese firms barred from selling cheap, memory chip prices rose, which critics said hurt American computer makers and buyers. Assessments of the deal are mixed: the US industry's later recovery is usually credited mainly to better technology and design, not to the agreement.

The winner who wasn't at the table

The biggest beneficiary was South Korea. While Japanese chip prices were being policed, Korean firms, led by Samsung Electronics, expanded fast.

World DRAM market share, 1987 vs 2001. Korea overtook Japan in 1998. Source: Wikipedia, 1986 U.S.–Japan Semiconductor Agreement.

Japan's share of the DRAM market fell from 75% in 1987 to 20% in 2001. Korea's rose from 5% to 40%, overtaking Japan in 1998.

Why it matters for India

Three lessons carry over. Tariffs often target finished goods, so consumers pay. Managed-trade targets are hard to enforce. And when two big economies fight, a third country that is ready to supply can end up the biggest winner. Keep that in mind when reading about the US–China trade war of 2018–19.
